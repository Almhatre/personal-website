<Entry id="photonium" title={<>Photonium, <em>cofounder & CTO</em></>}>

Photonium builds AI tools for optical design. Optical engineers spend most of
their day in Zemax or Code V nudging surface parameters, running merit
functions, and tolerancing existing designs. A good chunk of that work is
pattern-matching against an internal library of tricks, and that is the part
we automate.

Neutral-atom quantum computing, space instruments, lithography, and precision
measurement are all bottlenecked by optics. If optical design gets
substantially cheaper, those fields get easier.

</Entry>

<Entry title={<>Avalanche Energy, <em>computational physicist</em></>}>

I joined Avalanche for the summer to work on parameter-space exploration of
their reactor: pushing a simulation through thousands of configurations and
asking, at each point, what the plasma was doing physically, why, and how
that informed the next set of values to simulate.

You can't just throw data analysis at it. Most of my time went to individual
simulations in Paraview: the fields, the particle trajectories, where and
why losses happened. You reason physically about one regime, then decide
what to measure and what to run next.

</Entry>

<Entry title={<>Stanford XOC / KIPAC, <em>undergraduate researcher</em></>}>

Two years of work with an X-ray AGN catalogue under Dr. Meredith Powell. The
question: look at *where* active galactic nuclei sit, in the sky and in
redshift, and ask whether their spatial clustering tells us anything about
how supermassive black holes grew and co-evolved with the galaxies they live
in. *Where* things are turns out to be almost as informative as *what* they
are.

We used two complementary probes. The two-point correlation function, the
workhorse of clustering statistics, gives the excess probability of finding a
second AGN at a distance *r* from a first one:

<Eq num="1">
ξ(r) = ⟨ δ(**x**) δ(**x** + **r**) ⟩
</Eq>

Two-point statistics discard most of the information in a point field, so we
switched probes: for a given AGN, how far was its *k*-th nearest galaxy
neighbour? The *k*-nearest-neighbor CDF is sensitive to the full hierarchy of
*N*-point clustering, not just the two-point piece. It had been used on
galaxies and galaxy clusters; we were the first to apply it to AGN.

<Eq num="2">
CDF<sub>k</sub>(r) = P[ N<sub>gal</sub>( &lt; r ) ≥ k ]
</Eq>

The two probes gave the paper its claims. AGN hosting more massive black
holes have significantly closer galaxy neighbours than AGN hosting
less-massive ones, a trend earlier two-point measurements had only hinted at
around 2σ, and that *k*NN pulled up to 99.98% confidence. And *k*NN turned
out to be a genuinely complementary probe to the two-point function, seeing
structure the two-point statistic can't. [First-author paper →](/AGNClusteringPaper.pdf)

</Entry>
