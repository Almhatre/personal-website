Two years of work with an X-ray AGN catalogue, one first-author paper. I
learned how to **suspect a catalogue** — to look at a countless data points and
ask *which of you are lying to me?* — which turns out to be the most
transferable skill in science.

<Margin tag="reading">

<Body>

I started this work the summer after my freshman year and didn't stop for two
years. The raw material was an X-ray AGN catalogue — thousands of
active galactic nuclei, each one a flag that a supermassive black hole is,
right now, feeding. The question: look at *where* those flags sit, in the sky
and in redshift, and ask whether their spatial clustering tells us anything
about how supermassive black holes grew, and how they co-evolved with the
galaxies they sit inside.

It seems to me that *where* things are is almost as
informative as *what* they are. A population of black holes that all cluster
tightly is telling a different growth story than one that is flung evenly
across the sky. Clustering is a biography, written statistically.

Most of the two years was not ideas. It was catalogues with missing columns,
covariance matrix calculations that brought doubts to our conclusions, and many 
re-examinations of the same data. I first-authored a paper at the end of it.
It is on the arXiv and was published in Astronomy and Astrophysics (A&A). It is not famous.

<Eq num="1.1">
ξ(r) = ⟨ δ(**x**) δ(**x** + **r**) ⟩
</Eq>

The two-point correlation function: the excess probability of finding a second
AGN at a distance *r* from a first one, over what you'd expect at random. The
workhorse of clustering statistics.

Two-point statistics throw away most of the information in a point field. So we switched probes: rather than asking how AGN pair up
with each other, we asked how the *galaxies around each AGN* were laid out; in other words,
for a given AGN, how far was its *k*-th nearest galaxy neighbour? The
*k*-nearest-neighbor CDF is sensitive to the full hierarchy of *N*-point
clustering, not just the two-point piece. It had been used on galaxies and
galaxy clusters; nobody had turned it on AGN.

<Eq num="1.2">
CDF<sub>k</sub>(r) = P[ N<sub>gal</sub>( &lt; r ) ≥ k ]
</Eq>

The *k*-nearest-neighbor CDF: for a given AGN, the probability that at least
*k* galaxies sit within angular distance *r* of it. A single, simple statistic
that folds in every *N*-point moment at once.

The two probes, put together, gave the paper its two claims. **One:** AGN
hosting more massive supermassive black holes have significantly closer galaxy
neighbours than AGN hosting less-massive ones — a trend earlier 2-point
measurements had only hinted at around 2σ, and that *k*NN pulled up to 99.98%
confidence. **Two:** *k*NN is a genuinely complementary clustering probe to
the classical correlation function because it sees structure the two-point function
can't.
