I have played piano since I was five. My teacher then is my teacher now. She has watched me go from Mary Had a Little Lamb to Chopin Ballades. She is one of the most consistent presences in my life who is not family. Without her, I would be markedly less artistic, less introspective, less philosophical, and considerably more boring.

Most of our lessons are not, strictly, about playing. They are about *pushing on a piece*. What did the composer mean here? How much can we get away with? Play only the alto line. Now only the tenor. Now the inner voices muted, with the outer ones imagined. Each decomposition is a different probe of the same object, and each tells you something the last one didn't. The final interpretation is a superposition: what you heard when you took the piece apart, reassembled at performance.

It took me embarrassingly long to notice that this is exactly the move physics trains you to make. *Everything is an oscillator until it isn't.*<Footnote n="2" /> At small amplitude the response is linear and clean; the interesting physics (and the interesting music) lives in the non-quadratic terms. A harmonic reading of a piece is objectively correct: the right notes, the right tempo, no personality. The performance you remember is the one where the player found the amplitude at which the linear approximation breaks, and lived there without letting the whole system collapse. How much you can get away with is a question about where the quadratic approximation ends.

A pendulum is the clean case, so here's one. Drag the bob. At small angles the period doesn't care how hard you pulled; at large angles, it does. Piano is the same. Lifting is the same. The trick is knowing which regime you are actually in and, when it's worth it, choosing the nonlinear one on purpose.
